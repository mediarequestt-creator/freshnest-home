Enzyme Cleaner: What It Is and Where to Use It

Enzyme cleaners are one of the biggest cleaning trends right now — and for good reason. Instead of harsh chemicals, they use natural enzymes that break down grease, food and odors at the source. Here is what they are, where they work best, and where they do not.

What you need

  • An enzyme cleaner (store-bought, or a citrus-peel DIY version)
  • Microfiber cloths
  • A spray bottle (for DIY versions)
  • Rubber gloves for pet messes

How to use it

  1. Pet accidents: blot up as much as you can first, then soak the spot with enzyme cleaner and let it sit for 10–15 minutes before blotting again. This is the number one use — enzymes break down the proteins that cause lingering smells.
  2. Kitchen grease: spray greasy stovetops and range hoods, wait 5–10 minutes, then wipe. Enzymes dissolve the grease instead of pushing it around.
  3. Smelly drains: pour enzyme cleaner down a slow or smelly drain, leave it overnight, and flush with hot water in the morning.
  4. Laundry stains: pre-treat food, grass and sweat stains with enzyme cleaner before washing — check the label first, since enzymes need time to work.
  5. Bathroom mildew: spray, wait 10 minutes, scrub lightly and rinse. For heavy buildup, repeat rather than switching to bleach.
Good to know: Enzymes are living chemistry — they work slowly and they hate heat. Never use enzyme cleaner with hot water (it kills the enzymes) and never mix it with bleach or vinegar, which also deactivate them. Room-temperature water and patience are the whole secret.
Safety first: Do not use enzyme cleaners on wool, silk or leather — the same enzymes that break down stains can damage natural protein fibers. Always patch-test on a hidden spot first.

What to buy

  • Enzyme cleaner, multi-surface
  • Enzyme pet stain and odor remover
  • Microfiber cloth set
